# Why is there a chip shortage?

The 2020–2023 semiconductor shortage was not a single bottleneck. It was a stack of demand shocks, foundry allocation choices, and long lead times for new capacity. Consumer electronics demand rose during COVID-19 lockdowns while automakers cancelled wafer starts, then rushed back into a market that had already been reallocated to higher-margin devices. Mature-node microcontrollers used in cars were especially scarce because foundries had little spare 28–40 nm capacity and new fabs take years to come online.

## Demand shock and cancelled auto orders

Lockdowns shifted spending from services to electronics. Laptops, consoles, and networking gear absorbed foundry slots that had previously served mixed industrial and automotive demand. Automakers, running just-in-time inventories, cancelled orders when vehicle sales fell, then discovered that restarted demand could not reclaim those slots quickly. A few weeks of cancelled starts cascaded into factory shutdowns because OEM chip inventories were measured in days, not months.

## Capacity, geography, and disruptions

Leading-edge logic is concentrated at a small number of foundries, with TSMC accounting for a large share. That concentration made allocation decisions globally binding. Separate shocks removed supply at the same time: winter storms in Texas, a fire at a Renesas plant, and logistics congestion. Export controls and geopolitical risk further tightened foundry allocation for some customers.

Announced expansions in Arizona, Japan, and Germany increase geographic diversity, but they do not immediately relieve mature-node automotive supply. Government CHIPS-style subsidies change the investment case; they do not compress a 3–5 year fab construction cycle.

## Why automakers were hit hardest

Automotive chips are often mature-node MCUs rather than leading-edge GPUs. During the shortage, foundries allocated scarce capacity to higher-margin consumer devices. Automakers later dual-sourced MCUs and signed long-term capacity reservations. Analysts generally date a gradual rebalancing after 2023 as demand normalized and inventory was rebuilt, rather than a single “end date” for the shortage.

## Implications

The episode is better read as a capacity-allocation failure under long capital cycles than as a temporary shipping delay. Any later shortage in mature nodes can recur if demand spikes again and customers have not reserved capacity.
